A bit ago I mentioned technique, and this is a&nbsp; topic with so much depth I can't cover it all&nbsp;&nbsp; in this video but I think it can be underpinned&nbsp; with a single idea, making your mouse movements&nbsp;&nbsp; more efficient. This, on it's own is somewhat&nbsp; meaningless but it should become a bit more&nbsp;&nbsp; valuable after going through some of the aiming&nbsp; categories and seeing how it applies within them. Static clicking is, on its surface, the&nbsp; simplest category. And when I first saw&nbsp;&nbsp; a scenario like this I thought, how is&nbsp; this useful? It's just dots on a wall&nbsp;&nbsp; and it yet it's invaluable for developing&nbsp; and refining your flicks. When playing,&nbsp;&nbsp; try to make a fast initial flick which lands close&nbsp; to the target before making a smaller movement to&nbsp;&nbsp; correct for any errors made during your first.&nbsp; The simplicity of static is its strength,&nbsp;&nbsp; these two core movements, the flick and micro&nbsp; correction, appear everywhere in more advanced&nbsp;&nbsp; forms but by stripping all of that fluff&nbsp; away you can isolate the underlying motions. To link it back to that key idea there are 2 ways&nbsp; you should try to optimise your mouse movements.&nbsp;&nbsp; For both initial flicks and microadjustments&nbsp; you want to try and draw as straight of a line&nbsp;&nbsp; as possible between your starting point and the&nbsp; target. Curving or having jagged lines is a pretty&nbsp;&nbsp; common issue and something to try and smooth out&nbsp; sooner rather than later even if it comes at the&nbsp;&nbsp; cost of initial flick speed. The second thing to&nbsp; focus on is where your flick lands, obviously dead&nbsp;&nbsp; on is ideal but this isn't always possible and&nbsp; underflicking is far preferable to overflicking.&nbsp;&nbsp; This is because of the change in the direction&nbsp; of the force your applying when you overflick&nbsp;&nbsp; and have to correct backwards, it's a lot harder&nbsp; than just following through with the same motion. This concept is even more applicable to&nbsp; tracking, it just applies in different&nbsp;&nbsp; ways. One of the most common issues I see in&nbsp; tracking is overadjusting after the target&nbsp;&nbsp; changes directions. Both overshooting and landing&nbsp; on the far side of the target are suboptimal here&nbsp;&nbsp; since even if you do land on target you are&nbsp; still moving your mouse more than you have to,&nbsp;&nbsp; increasing the amount of distance the target&nbsp; appears to travel relative to your crosshair&nbsp;&nbsp; making their movements harder to read. This often&nbsp; leads to chaining these overadjustments together&nbsp;&nbsp; which leads to this feeling of always lagging&nbsp; behind what the targets actually doing. The&nbsp;&nbsp; easiest way to avoid this is to try to land&nbsp; on the middle or inner edge of the target,&nbsp;&nbsp; by minimising you mouse movement you make&nbsp; it easier to read the target's and when it&nbsp;&nbsp; changes directions again you'll remain on&nbsp; it for longer before having to readjust. Dynamic clicking almost blends both of these&nbsp; because it requires clean flicks to first reach&nbsp;&nbsp; the target, clean microadjustments when needed&nbsp; and good movement reading skills to tell where&nbsp;&nbsp; the target is going to end up after you flick to&nbsp; it. Having straight lines and not overshooting&nbsp;&nbsp; helps with movement reading as well, combining&nbsp; the skills needed in the previous 2 categories&nbsp;&nbsp; and again, minimising mouse movement is&nbsp; what is needed to have good technique in&nbsp;&nbsp; all 3.
